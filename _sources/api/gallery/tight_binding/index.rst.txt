Tight-Binding
-------------

The generic real-space and reciprocal-space machinery: the square
lattice, whose single band is simple enough to check by hand, as the
smallest complete illustration of Bloch's theorem; Brillouin zones and
the standard high-symmetry paths of the 2D and 3D Bravais lattices;
Monkhorst-Pack k-point grids; a graphene flake
diagonalized directly and its Bloch band structure along a k-path,
including Wallace's 1947 linear (Dirac) dispersion near the K point;
the physics behind the 2004 isolation of graphene -- flakes, edge
states, and the Berry phase of pi of its Dirac cones; the zigzag edge
states of graphene ribbons and the 3m + 2 rule of armchair ribbons;
surface Green's functions by Lopez Sancho decimation; and
:class:`tbkit.plot.Plot`'s lattice, spectrum, density-of-states, and
eigenstate-intensity plots; parametrized Hamiltonians, whose value
functions let a flux or dimerization sweep re-evaluate the hoppings
instead of rebuilding the model; periodic boundaries in real space,
tori and cylinders whose spectra match the Bloch bands on a k-mesh;
and the analysis of a band structure: Fermi surfaces and their Lifshitz
transitions, bands projected on sublattices or spins and spin textures,
and the tetrahedron method for the density of states.

Brillouin (1930) gave the zone that carries his name: the set of \mathbf{k} closer to the origin than to any other reciprocal lattice vector \mathbf{G}, the Wigner-Seitz cell of the reciprocal lattice. Its faces are the planes where Bragg reflection |\mathbf{k}| = |\mathbf{k} - \mathbf{G}| opens band gaps, and its corners and face centres are the points of highest symmetry, where bands reach their extrema, cross or are degenerate. A band structure is therefore drawn along a path through them. W. Setyawan and S. Curtarolo (2010) fixed one standard path per Bravais lattice, so that band structures from different codes can be compared at a glance.">

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Total energies, charge densities and every ground-state property of a crystal are averages over the Brillouin zone, \bar f = \frac{1}{V_{BZ}}\int f(\mathbf{k})\,d\mathbf{k}. H. J. Monkhorst and J. D. Pack (1976) replaced the integral by the mean over a uniform grid of q points along each reciprocal vector, u_r = (2r - q - 1)/2q, r = 1, \dots, q. A smooth periodic function is a Fourier series f(\mathbf{k}) = \sum_\mathbf{R} f_\mathbf{R}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{R}}, and such a grid averages every term to zero except those with all R_i multiples of q: it integrates exactly every Fourier component of range below q lattice vectors. For an insulator the components decay exponentially, and so does the error. For even q the grid is shifted off \Gamma, and its points fall into fewer orbits of the point group, so fewer have to be computed. Monkhorst-Pack grids have been the default k-point sampling of band-structure codes ever since.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="A sweep over a magnetic field, a gate voltage or a dimerization usually rebuilds the model at every step: find the bonds, set the hoppings, apply the field. A value function replaces a number by a callable of the sites and of named parameters, t(site_i, site_j, params) or onsite(site, params). The model stores its bonds once, and get_ham (``sys.get_ham(alpha=...)``) or get_ham (``ks.get_ham(k, v=...)``) only re-evaluates the values. site_i and site_j are structured arrays holding every bond at once (fields &#x27;x&#x27;, &#x27;y&#x27;, &#x27;tag&#x27;, &#x27;index&#x27;), so each function runs once per Hamiltonian, vectorized.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="A surface is a crystal cut in half: periodic along the surface, but semi-infinite in depth. Its local density of states is -\frac{1}{\pi}\mathrm{Im}\,G_{00}(E + i\eta), the Green&#x27;s function of the outermost layer, which depends on every layer below it. A finite slab only approximates it, with discrete levels and a second surface. The recursion g = [E - h_0 - v\,g\,v^\dagger]^{-1}, which adds one layer at a time, converges only as fast as the broadening \eta damps the waves coming back from the bulk: \sim1/\eta steps.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="M. Fujita, K. Wakabayashi, K. Nakada and K. Kusakabe (1996) cut graphene into ribbons and found that the shape of the edge decides the low-energy physics. A ribbon with zigzag edges has a band of states at exactly E = 0, localized at the edges, over a third of its Brillouin zone, 2\pi/3 \le |ka| \le \pi. Each such state lives on one sublattice only, with amplitude (-2\cos\tfrac{ka}{2})^n on the n-th zigzag chain from its edge: localized when |2\cos\tfrac{ka}{2}| &lt; 1, most strongly at the zone boundary. The flat band piles up states at the Fermi level, a peak in the density of states that makes the edges magnetic once electrons interact. A ribbon with armchair edges has no edge states. Nakada et al. showed that it is metallic only when its number N of dimer lines is N = 3m + 2, and gapped otherwise.">
