\section*{Abstract}
{\bf
A tight-binding study often needs a model's bands, its topological
invariants, its linear response and the transport through a device cut
from it, and each step usually needs a different package with its own
conventions. tbkit is an open-source Python package in which one model
definition serves all of them. It computes band structures, Chern,
$\mathbb{Z}_2$, Bott and higher-order invariants, Kubo response, and
scattering matrices from exact lead modes, and it covers Floquet and
non-Hermitian systems, Wannier functions and mean-field interactions.
Every result stated in its documentation is asserted in continuous
integration, and tbkit agrees with Kwant and PythTB to within $10^{-12}$
on transmissions, lead modes, Berry phases and Wannier centres. We
benchmark it against both. tbkit installs from PyPI and
conda-forge. Its source is at \url{https://github.com/cpoli/tbkit}, and its
documentation, with the gallery, at \url{https://cpoli.github.io/tbkit}.
}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Overview and scope}

This paper introduces tbkit, a Python package that builds and solves
tight-binding models in one, two and three dimensions, written in
vectorized NumPy~\cite{numpy} and SciPy~\cite{scipy}. One model definition
serves both finite lattices and Bloch Hamiltonians, and the same model
can be carried from its bands and topological invariants through its
linear response to the transport through a device cut from it. The
package is built around verification: every numerical claim of its
documentation is asserted by code that runs in continuous integration
(CI), and its results agree with Kwant~\cite{groth2014} and
PythTB~\cite{pythtb} to near machine precision.

This document is the userguide of tbkit release
\tbkitversion~\cite{tbkit062}, and the reference to cite when tbkit is
used in research. It gives the background and the added value of the
package (this section), describes its design and algorithms
(Secs.~\ref{sec:design} to~\ref{sec:beyond}), explains how to install,
test and use it (Sec.~\ref{sec:usage}), and works through a complete
example (Sec.~\ref{sec:example}). It then validates the results against
independent packages and closed-form results (Sec.~\ref{sec:validation})
and measures performance (Sec.~\ref{sec:performance}). The online
documentation complements it with the full API reference and a gallery of
107 examples, and grows with each release.

\subsection{Background and motivation}

Tight-binding models are among the most widely used tools of condensed-matter physics. They
describe the bands of graphene and of transition-metal dichalcogenides,
quantum Hall and topological insulators~\cite{tknn1982,hasan2010}, disordered conductors and
mesoscopic devices, and they are the reference language for teaching band
theory. Several mature packages serve parts of this landscape.
Kwant~\cite{groth2014} computes quantum transport through large devices.
PythTB~\cite{pythtb} and Z2Pack~\cite{gresch2017} focus on Berry phases and
topological invariants, and TBmodels~\cite{gresch2018} and
WannierTools~\cite{wu2018} work with Wannier90~\cite{pizzi2020}
Hamiltonians of real materials. pybinding~\cite{pybinding} targets large
real-space systems with the kernel polynomial method.

A typical project, however, crosses these boundaries. A researcher studying
a Chern insulator wants its bands, its Chern number, its Hall conductivity
at arbitrary filling, the edge states of a ribbon, and the conductance of
a device cut from the same model, possibly with disorder or a periodic
drive. With separate packages, the model has to be rewritten for each, in
different conventions for the Bloch gauge, the hopping direction or the
sign of the Hall conductivity. Each rewrite can introduce errors.

Earlier versions of tbkit served the author's research at Lancaster
University, on strain-induced pseudo-Landau levels in
graphene~\cite{poli2014,bellec2020}, topological interface and defect
states in microwave resonator lattices~\cite{poli2015,poli2017}, and the
sign of couplings in photonic tight-binding lattices~\cite{keil2016}.
Release \tbkitversion{} grew from that code into a general package.

tbkit addresses this with a single model definition and a broad
set of tools that act on it (Sec.~\ref{sec:design}). Release
\tbkitversion{} supports
\begin{enumerate}
\item finite lattices of any shape and Bloch Hamiltonians built from the
  same hoppings, with conversions between them, ribbons and slabs;
\item several orbitals and spin per site, Slater--Koster parameters,
  overlaps, discretized $k\cdot p$ models and Wannier90 imports;
\item disorder, defects, strain and magnetic fields through Peierls phases;
\item topological invariants in reciprocal space (Chern, spin and mirror
  Chern, $\mathbb{Z}_2$, Fu--Kane parities, Weyl-point chiralities,
  higher-order and Majorana invariants) and in real space (local Chern
  marker, Bott index);
\item Kubo response at any Fermi level and temperature: anomalous and
  spin Hall, anomalous Nernst and thermal Hall conductivities, orbital
  magnetization, the axion angle, and linear and nonlinear optics;
\item scattering matrices from exact lead modes, with conservation laws,
  scattering states, finite temperature and thermoelectric coefficients,
  and Green's-function transport for multi-terminal devices;
\item Floquet drives and non-Hermitian bands, with the anomalous Floquet
  winding, biorthogonal Chern numbers, the skin effect and exceptional
  points;
\item maximally localized Wannier functions, Hubbard and
  Bogoliubov--de Gennes mean field, and moir\'e supercells;
\item lattices of $10^5$ sites and more, through sparse diagonalization,
  the kernel polynomial method and recursive Green's functions.
\end{enumerate}
Its added value over existing packages lies in four points. First, one
model definition serves every tool, so the bands, invariants, response
and transport of a model are computed with one set of conventions
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:bloch} below) and no rewriting. Second, scattering matrices
from exact lead modes, as in Kwant, sit in the same package as Berry-phase
topology and Kubo response, and a lead is cut automatically from the
Bloch model of the device. Third, non-Hermitian and Floquet models are
first-class: exceptional points are found from the discriminant of the
characteristic polynomial and followed by eigenvalue continuation, and
every band and topology tool applies to a Floquet Hamiltonian. Fourth,
verification is part of the design: every number that the documentation
states is asserted by code on every commit.
Table~\ref{tab:packages} compares this scope with the two packages tbkit
is validated against. We do not claim it is the fastest package for any
one task: Kwant scales further for large transport calculations, and
PythTB~2 runs within a factor of about three of tbkit on Bloch meshes
(Sec.~\ref{sec:performance}). What tbkit provides is breadth in one
package, with mechanics that a user can inspect, and verification
throughout (Sec.~\ref{sec:validation}).

\subsection{Links, documentation and reporting}

tbkit is distributed under the BSD 3-clause license on PyPI and
conda-forge (installation in Sec.~\ref{sec:usage}). The source code is at
\url{https://github.com/cpoli/tbkit}, and the documentation, with the API
reference, the gallery and its coverage report, at
\url{https://cpoli.github.io/tbkit}. Bugs and feature requests go to the
issue tracker of the repository. Every script behind this paper, from
validation and benchmarks to figures and listings, is in the
\code{paper/} directory of the repository, and \code{paper/README.md}
gives the commands that regenerate every number, figure and table.

Each release is a git tag. A release workflow builds it, publishes it to
PyPI, and archives it on Zenodo under its own DOI. The changelog records
every change, and for each fixed bug it states which numbers were wrong
and why. Contributions arrive as pull requests and pass the same CI as
the rest of the code (Sec.~\ref{sec:design}), so a contribution that
changes the physics of any documented result fails before it is merged.

\label{sec:design}

\paragraph{Two pipelines.} A \code{Lattice} holds a unit cell (orbital
positions and one-character sublattice tags) and primitive vectors
(Fig.~\ref{fig:design}). In real space, \code{get\_lattice} repeats the cell, geometry methods
cut flakes, discs or ribbons, and a \code{System} sets hoppings by neighbour
order, angle or sublattice pair. Modifiers then act on the stored
hoppings: disorder, defects, strain, and Peierls phases for magnetic fields.
\code{get\_ham} assembles a sparse matrix. In reciprocal space, a
\code{KSpace} takes hoppings as an orbital pair and a lattice offset
$\mathbf{R}$ and returns $H(\mathbf{k})$. Every specialized model returns a
\code{KSpace}, so every band and topology tool applies to it. These include
magnetic supercells, Slater--Koster multi-orbital models~\cite{slater1954},
Bogoliubov--de Gennes Hamiltonians, Floquet Hamiltonians, moir\'e
supercells, discretized $k\cdot p$ models and Wannier90 imports.

\paragraph{One Bloch sum, one eigensolver.} Every Bloch matrix in the package
comes from one vectorized routine (\code{KSpace.\_bloch\_sum}). This holds
for the Hamiltonian, overlaps, velocities $\partial_\mu H$, vector-potential
couplings and Floquet grids. The routine evaluates many $\mathbf{k}$ at once,
with optional analytic derivatives. It groups the hoppings by lattice
vector into dense blocks $T_{\mathbf{R}}$, so that $H(\mathbf{k})$ at all
k-points is one matrix product of the phases $e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{R}}$
with these blocks, and the orbital positions enter as phases of the rows
and columns. For sparse models, such as large moir\'e supercells, it
sums the hoppings one by one instead. Every diagonalization over a mesh
goes through one stacked eigensolver (\code{KSpace.\_eigs}), in
memory-bounded chunks and optionally across threads. Optimizing or
correcting these two routines therefore affects every method built on
them.

\paragraph{Conventions.} A hopping $t_{ij}(\mathbf{R})$ from orbital $j$
in the cell at lattice vector $\mathbf{R}$ to orbital $i$ in the home cell
enters the Bloch matrix as
\begin{equation}
H_{ij}(\mathbf{k}) = \varepsilon_i\,\delta_{ij}
  + \sum_{\mathbf{R}} t_{ij}(\mathbf{R})\, e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{d}_{ij}(\mathbf{R})}\,,
\qquad
\mathbf{d}_{ij}(\mathbf{R}) = \mathbf{R}
\quad\text{or}\quad
\mathbf{R} + \boldsymbol{\tau}_j - \boldsymbol{\tau}_i\,,
\label{eq:bloch}
\end{equation}
with $\varepsilon_i$ the onsite energies and $\boldsymbol{\tau}_i$ the
orbital positions. By default $H(\mathbf{k})$ uses the periodic gauge,
$\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{R}$, which leaves eigenvalues and integrated
invariants unchanged. Wilson loops, Wannier centres and the quantum metric
add the positions back when requested (\code{positions=True}), which is
the convention of PythTB. The derivatives $\partial_\mu H$ follow
analytically by multiplying each term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:bloch} by
$i d_\mu$. The Hall conductivity follows the sign of Thouless,
Kohmoto, Nightingale and den Nijs (TKNN)~\cite{tknn1982}, so in a gap
it equals the Chern number returned by \code{chern\_number}. The
conventions are stated in the docstrings and pinned by tests.

\paragraph{Value functions.} Hoppings and onsite energies may be functions
of the sites and of named parameters, \code{t(site\_i, site\_j, **params)}.
\code{get\_ham(**params)} evaluates them, so a parameter sweep or a phase
diagram reuses one model, as with Kwant's value functions.

\paragraph{Validation.} Each public method first validates its arguments
through the 253 checks of one module, \code{error\_handling}. A failed
check raises an explicit error naming the problem, for example
the missing lead orbitals of an incomplete interface cell, or a band group of
a non-Hermitian model without a line gap.

\paragraph{Verification policy.} The test suite runs on Python 3.10--3.14,
plus a job on the oldest NumPy, SciPy and matplotlib the package declares.
It covers every line, and any warning fails it. A fixed bug gets a test
that pins it. The 107 gallery examples are executed by every documentation
build, which CI runs on each push, and each asserts its key numbers before
plotting. A change that breaks the physics of an example therefore fails
CI. Section~\ref{sec:validation} adds independent checks against Kwant and
PythTB.

\section{Building models}
\label{sec:building}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_models}
\caption{Building and solving models.
(a)~An impurity binds a state below the band of a graphene flake.
(b)~Slater--Koster $sp^3$ bands of graphene, with overlaps (a generalized
eigenproblem). (c)~Kernel-polynomial density of states of a 45\,000-site
lattice, clean and with 2\% vacancies.}
\label{fig:models}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Lattices and geometry.} Ready-made lattices (chain, square,
triangular, honeycomb, kagome, Lieb) and arbitrary unit cells in 1D, 2D
and 3D are supported. Geometry operations remove sites, cut by lines or
ellipses, rotate, and close the boundaries into a torus or cylinder. On a
torus, every bond vector uses the minimum-image convention. Neighbour
search switches from dense distances to a $k$-d tree above 5000 sites.

\paragraph{Several orbitals and spin.} \code{OrbitalSystem} attaches
orbitals ($s$, $p$, $d$) and spin to each site, and builds hopping blocks
from Slater--Koster parameters, intrinsic and Rashba spin--orbit coupling,
or explicit matrices [Fig.~\ref{fig:models}(b)]. Overlaps turn every
eigenproblem into a generalized one.

\paragraph{From continuum to lattice.} \code{discretize} turns a $k\cdot p$
Hamiltonian, written in SymPy~\cite{sympy}, into a lattice model on a chain, square or
cubic grid. Its parameters can stay symbolic and become value functions.

\paragraph{Wannier functions.} \code{read\_wannier90} imports
\code{\_hr.dat} models. \code{wannierize} builds maximally localized Wannier
functions of any \code{KSpace} model~\cite{marzari1997}, with
Souza--Marzari--Vanderbilt disentanglement for entangled bands~\cite{souza2001}. Its result
interpolates the bands through a Wannier-basis \code{KSpace}.

\paragraph{Large systems.} Sparse shift-invert diagonalization, the kernel
polynomial method~\cite{weisse2006} [Fig.~\ref{fig:models}(c)], and a
recursive Green's function for long quasi-one-dimensional devices
extend real-space calculations to $10^5$ sites and more.

\section{Band topology}
\label{sec:topology}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_topology}
\caption{Band topology. (a)~Berry curvature of the lower band of the
Haldane model over the Brillouin zone. (b)~Wilson-loop (hybrid Wannier
centre) flow of the occupied pair of the Kane--Mele model~\cite{kane2005qsh}: the centres
wind, so $\mathbb{Z}_2 = 1$. (c)~Corner states of a finite quadrupole
insulator.}
\label{fig:topology}
\end{figure}

Chern numbers use the gauge-invariant plaquette method of Fukui, Hatsugai and
Suzuki~\cite{fukui2005}. With $M_{mn}(\mathbf{k}, \mathbf{k}') =
\langle u_m(\mathbf{k})|u_n(\mathbf{k}')\rangle$ the overlaps of the
occupied Bloch states between neighbouring mesh points, the Berry flux
through a plaquette with corners $\mathbf{k}_1, \dots, \mathbf{k}_4$ and the
Chern number are
\begin{equation}
\Omega_\square = -\arg\prod_{a=1}^{4}\det M(\mathbf{k}_a, \mathbf{k}_{a+1})\,,
\qquad
C = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{\square}\Omega_\square\,,
\label{eq:fhs}
\end{equation}
with $\mathbf{k}_5 = \mathbf{k}_1$. Each flux lies in $(-\pi, \pi]$ and
is independent of the phases of the states, so on a coarse mesh $C$ is an
integer whenever the gap stays open [Fig.~\ref{fig:topology}(a)]. Spin and mirror
Chern numbers project onto the eigenspaces of $s_z$ or of a mirror operator
first. Wilson loops give Berry and Zak phases~\cite{xiao2010}, hybrid Wannier centres and
their flow~\cite{soluyanov2011}, from which the $\mathbb{Z}_2$ invariant~\cite{kane2005z2}
is read in 2D [Fig.~\ref{fig:topology}(b)] and the four 3D indices
follow. Inversion-symmetric models also give Fu--Kane
parities~\cite{fu2007}. In real space, tbkit computes the local Chern
marker~\cite{bianco2011}, the Bott index~\cite{loring2010} and the
entanglement spectrum, so disordered or finite samples need no
translation symmetry. Nested Wilson loops and corner charges diagnose
higher-order phases~\cite{benalcazar2017} [Fig.~\ref{fig:topology}(c)].
\code{find\_weyl\_points} locates Weyl nodes and their chiralities from
the flux through small spheres. For superconductors, \code{bdg} gives
Kitaev's Majorana number from the Pfaffian~\cite{kitaev2001}, and the
symmetry tools classify a Hamiltonian in the tenfold way~\cite{altland1997,chiu2016}.

\section{Response and transport}
\label{sec:response}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_response}
\caption{Response and transport. (a)~Anomalous Hall conductivity of the
Haldane model against the Fermi level: a plateau at $C=1$ in the gap of the
topological phase, a non-quantized value in the bands. (b)~Conductance
steps of a quantum point contact. (c)~Optical conductivity of graphene,
which approaches the universal value $\sigma_0 = e^2/4\hbar$ at low
frequency~\cite{nair2008}.}
\label{fig:response}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Kubo response.} The anomalous Hall conductivity is evaluated
from the Kubo formula at any Fermi level and temperature,
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{xy} = \frac{e^2}{\hbar}\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}\frac{d^dk}{(2\pi)^d}
\sum_{n\neq m} -(w_n - w_m)\,
\frac{\mathrm{Im}\left[\langle n|\partial_x H|m\rangle\langle m|\partial_y H|n\rangle\right]}
{(E_n - E_m)^2}\,,
\label{eq:kubo}
\end{equation}
with the weights $w_n = f(E_n)$ given by the Fermi--Dirac occupations and
the velocities $\partial_\mu H$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:bloch}
[Fig.~\ref{fig:response}(a)]. In a gap, Eq.~\eqref{eq:kubo} equals
$C\,e^2/h$ with $C$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:fhs}. Written with $w_n - w_m$
rather than as a sum of band Berry curvatures, it stays finite where
bands touch, since degenerate pairs have equal weights and drop out. An
optional adaptive refinement resamples the cells where the integrand
peaks. The spin Hall conductivity replaces $\partial_x H$ by the spin
current. The anomalous Nernst and thermal Hall conductivities and the
orbital magnetization share the same code, with the entropy or
higher moments of the occupations as weights~\cite{xiao2010}, and the
axion angle integrates the second Chern form along a model parameter. Linear optical conductivity, the
Berry-curvature dipole~\cite{sodemann2015} and the shift current~\cite{sipe2000} cover optics
[Fig.~\ref{fig:response}(c)]. In real space, the kernel polynomial method
evaluates the Kubo--Bastin formula~\cite{garcia2015} on large disordered tori.

\paragraph{Scattering matrix.} \code{Transport} holds a sparse device and
its leads. A lead is given as matrices or attached automatically from a
ribbon \code{KSpace}, by matching positions and tags. A lead with unit-cell
Hamiltonian $h_0$ and inter-cell hopping $v$ carries modes
$\psi_{n+1} = \lambda\,\psi_n$, which solve the pencil
\begin{equation}
\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -v^\dagger & E - h_0 \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} \psi_{n-1} \\ \psi_n \end{pmatrix}
= \lambda
\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & v \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} \psi_{n-1} \\ \psi_n \end{pmatrix}.
\label{eq:pencil}
\end{equation}
tbkit solves it as an ordinary eigenproblem when $v$ is well conditioned,
and with an ordered QZ decomposition otherwise. Modes with $|\lambda| = 1$
propagate with velocity $-2\,\mathrm{Im}(\lambda\,\phi^\dagger v\,\phi)$
and are normalized to unit current. Degenerate modes are rotated to
diagonalize the velocity. Evanescent modes are kept in an ordered Schur
basis, which stays well conditioned when $v$ is singular. In lead $l$,
coupled to the device by $\tau_l$, the wave function on its first two cells
is an incoming mode $\phi^{\mathrm{in}}_l$ plus $U_{0,l}b_l$ and
$U_{1,l}b_l$, where $U_{0,l}$ and $U_{1,l}$ hold the outgoing and
evanescent solutions. The device wave function $\psi$ and the amplitudes
$b_l$ then solve one sparse system,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
(E - H)\psi - \sum_l\tau_l U_{0,l}b_l &= \sum_l\tau_l\phi^{\mathrm{in}}_l\,,\\
-\tau_l^\dagger\psi + \left[(E - h_0)U_{0,l} - vU_{1,l}\right]b_l
  &= -(E - h_0 - \lambda_{\mathrm{in}}v)\,\phi^{\mathrm{in}}_l\,,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:scattering}
\end{equation}
factorized once by sparse LU~\cite{li2005} for every incoming mode, as in
Kwant~\cite{groth2014}. The outgoing entries of $b_l$ form the columns of
the scattering matrix $S$, and the transmission is
$T = \sum |S_{nm}|^2$ over modes $m$ of the injecting lead and $n$ of the
receiving one. Because no broadening $\eta$ enters, results stay exact up
to the band edges of the leads. A conservation law, such as $\sigma_z$ or the
electron--hole $\tau_z$, splits the modes into blocks for spin-resolved
transport or Andreev reflection. Scattering states, their local density of
states, finite temperature and thermoelectric coefficients build on the
same factorization [Fig.~\ref{fig:response}(b)].

\paragraph{Green's functions.} Caroli formulas with Sancho--Rubio
leads~\cite{sancho1985} give multi-terminal conductance matrices, bond
currents and shot noise. \code{surface\_spectral\_function} gives the
spectral function of a semi-infinite crystal, for example the Dirac cone on
the surface of a 3D topological insulator.

\section{Driven, open and interacting systems}
\label{sec:beyond}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_driven}
\caption{Driven and open systems. (a)~Bloch oscillations of a wavepacket
in a tilted chain. (b)~Floquet bands of graphene under circularly
polarized light, gapped at $K$~\cite{oka2009}. (c)~Skin effect in the Hatano--Nelson
chain~\cite{hatano1996}: the spectrum of a ring winds around a loop, while the open chain

\label{fig:driven}

\paragraph{Floquet.} Any $H(\mathbf{k}, t)$ becomes a Floquet
\code{KSpace} whose Hamiltonian is $H_F(\mathbf{k})$, so every band and
topology tool applies to it [Fig.~\ref{fig:driven}(b)]. Step drives are
exact. The winding number of Rudner \emph{et al.}~\cite{rudner2013}
identifies anomalous phases that the Chern numbers of $H_F$ miss.

\paragraph{Non-Hermitian bands.} Non-reciprocal hoppings, gain and loss are
first-class~\cite{bergholtz2021}. Biorthogonal Berry curvature and Chern numbers apply to
line-gapped bands, and the spectral winding and generalized Brillouin
zone~\cite{yao2018,yokomizo2019} describe the skin effect [Fig.~\ref{fig:driven}(c)]. The module
\code{exceptional} follows eigenvalues by continuation along a closed loop,
without sorting them. From this it computes vorticities and the band swap
on encircling an exceptional point, and finds exceptional points and their
charges from the discriminant of the characteristic polynomial.

\paragraph{Interactions and dynamics.} Hubbard mean field (collinear and
non-collinear) and the self-consistent Bogoliubov--de Gennes gap equation
work on finite systems. Twisted-bilayer supercells with band unfolding
reach the flat bands at the magic angle~\cite{bistritzer2011}.
Crank--Nicolson propagation follows wavepackets and adiabatic pumping
[Fig.~\ref{fig:driven}(a)].

\section{Using tbkit}
\label{sec:usage}

\subsection{Installation and tests}

tbkit runs on Python~3.10 and later. It requires NumPy, SciPy and
matplotlib~\cite{matplotlib}, and optionally SymPy~\cite{sympy} for
$k\cdot p$ discretization. A release installs with either of
\begin{verbatim}
pip install tbkit
conda install -c conda-forge tbkit
\end{verbatim}
and the development version, with the test dependencies, from the
repository:
\begin{verbatim}
git clone https://github.com/cpoli/tbkit
cd tbkit
pip install -e ".[test]"
pytest tests/
\end{verbatim}
The last command runs the 752 tests in parallel in about half a minute on
a laptop, and \code{pytest tests/ --cov=tbkit} also reports their line
coverage, as CI does. \code{pip install -e ".[docs]"} followed by
\code{make html} in \code{docs/} builds the documentation, which re-runs
and asserts every gallery example. The cross-checks against Kwant and
PythTB and the benchmarks of Secs.~\ref{sec:validation}
and~\ref{sec:performance} need both packages, which
\code{paper/environment.yml} installs, and \code{paper/README.md} gives
the commands that run them.

\subsection{Basic workflow}

\lstinputlisting[caption={A first model, in real space and in reciprocal
space. Every step asserts its result.}, label=lst:quickstart,
firstline=2]{../listings/quickstart.py}

Listing~\ref{lst:quickstart} shows the two pipelines of
Fig.~\ref{fig:design}. In real space (step~1), a \code{Lattice} is
repeated into a finite patch, a \code{System} addresses its bonds by
neighbour order (\code{'n': 1}), and \code{get\_ham} and \code{get\_eig}
build and diagonalize the sparse Hamiltonian. The levels of the
$20\times20$ flake are checked against their closed form. In reciprocal
space (step~2), a \code{KSpace} takes each bond as an orbital pair and the
lattice vector $\mathbf{R}$ of the target cell, adds its Hermitian
conjugate, and returns $H(\mathbf{k})$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:bloch}.
\code{high\_symmetry\_path} recognizes the Bravais lattice and returns a
standard path, along which \code{k\_path} gives the bands. Energies are in
the units of the hoppings, lengths in those of the primitive vectors, and
conductances in $e^2/h$.

From there, every tool of Secs.~\ref{sec:building} to~\ref{sec:beyond} is
a method of these objects or a function that takes them.
Table~\ref{tab:entry} lists the main entry points. Each public function
and method has a docstring with its parameters, conventions and
references, rendered in the API reference of the documentation, and a
wrong argument raises an error that names the problem. The gallery
gives a runnable, self-checking script for each topic, and a tutorial
page walks through the package in the order of this guide.

\section{A worked example: the Haldane model, from bands to transport}
\label{sec:example}

Listing~\ref{lst:haldane} follows one model, Haldane's Chern
insulator~\cite{haldane1988}, through the whole package. The listing is
the file \code{paper/listings/haldane\_workflow.py} of the repository, which
CI runs, so the code printed here is code that passes.

\lstinputlisting[caption={The Haldane model from its bands to its
conductance. Every step asserts its result.}, label=lst:haldane,
firstline=2]{../listings/haldane_workflow.py}

The model is built once (step 1), from graphene's three nearest-neighbour
bonds and the complex second-neighbour hopping $\pm i t_2$. Step 2 checks
the gaps at the two valleys, $2|M \mp 3\sqrt3\,t_2|$. Step 3 computes the
Chern number of the lower band, $C = +1$, and the Hall conductivity with
the Fermi level in the gap, $\sigma_{xy} = C\,e^2/h$, from two independent
algorithms: plaquette fluxes and the Kubo formula. Step 4 cuts a ribbon
from the same hopping list and finds states in the bulk gap. Step 5 turns
the Bloch model into a finite device (\code{finite\_system}), attaches the
ribbon as leads on both sides, and finds the transmission $T = 1$ carried by
the single chiral edge channel. The whole script runs in under a second on
a laptop.

\section{Validation}
\label{sec:validation}

We validate tbkit at three levels.

\paragraph{Against Kwant.} We build three devices independently in tbkit and in
Kwant~\kwantversion, on identical site positions:
a square-lattice strip with a smooth antidot, a disordered graphene device
with zigzag-ribbon leads, and a strip with Rashba spin--orbit coupling and
spin-conserving leads. We compare gauge-invariant quantities: the momenta
and velocities of the lead modes, the transmission, the transmission
eigenvalues (squared singular values of $t$), and the four spin-resolved
blocks of $S$. The comparison covers 37 to 53 energies per device.

\paragraph{Against PythTB.} We build the Haldane and Kane--Mele models with
PythTB~\pythtbversion's own model library and carry them into tbkit
through their hopping lists only. Each package then computes band energies,
Chern numbers, Berry phases, the quantum metric and Berry curvature, and
hybrid Wannier centres on its own. tbkit's $\mathbb{Z}_2$ invariant
reproduces the phase diagram: $\mathbb{Z}_2 = 1$ in the quantum spin Hall
phase and 0 in the trivial phase.

On Bloch meshes, tbkit and PythTB~2 scale alike [Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmarks}(a)--(c)].
Both are vectorized over $k$ and build $H(\mathbf{k})$ by grouping the
hoppings by lattice vector (Sec.~\ref{sec:design}), and their run times
stay within a factor of about three. tbkit is 1.2 to 2.9 times faster for
the Chern number, and 1.1 to 1.4 times faster for band energies except
on the smallest two-orbital meshes. For Wilson loops, PythTB is up to 1.8
times faster on small meshes, and tbkit is 1.1 to 1.2 times faster on the
$200\times200$ mesh with 8 and 32 orbitals. tbkit's chunked eigensolver bounds
its memory, which is 5 to 75 times lower for 32 orbitals on the largest
mesh. It can also use threads
(\code{KSpace.set\_workers}), which this single-thread comparison leaves
out. For transport, tbkit is as fast as Kwant up to a few thousand
sites. Beyond that, Kwant's MUMPS factorization~\cite{amestoy2001}, with its nested-dissection
ordering, pulls ahead of tbkit's SuperLU~\cite{li2005}, and the gap widens slowly with size
[Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmarks}(d)]. At about $10^5$ sites, Kwant is 2.3 times
faster, and at about $4\times10^5$ sites 2.7 times faster, using about a
quarter of the memory. For the largest devices, Kwant is the right tool.
tbkit's transport is aimed at the models that a user already builds for
its band and topology tools, and at devices of up to about $10^5$ sites,
where it takes under 6 seconds on one thread.

\section{Teaching}
\label{sec:teaching}

tbkit was written to be read as well as used. The gallery spans 21 topics,
from lattices to Floquet and non-Hermitian physics, and each example is a short
script that explains its physics and checks it. A history page of the
documentation tells the story of tight-binding physics as a chronology of
87 breakthroughs, from Bloch and Slater--Koster to moir\'e flat bands. Each
breakthrough links to the examples that reproduce it, 95 examples in all,
and no example is shared between two breakthroughs. The
two pipelines expose their intermediate objects (site arrays, bond tables,
sparse Hamiltonians), so a student can see how a model is assembled.

\section{Summary and future directions}
\label{sec:conclusion}

tbkit lets one tight-binding model be carried from its bands and
topological invariants through its linear response to the transport of a
device cut from it, with one set of conventions. Its results agree with
Kwant and PythTB to near machine precision, its gallery reproduces
closed-form results from the literature and asserts them on every commit,
and its performance is on par with or better than PythTB on Bloch meshes and on par with Kwant
for devices of up to a few thousand sites. For the largest transport
calculations, Kwant remains the better choice.

The roadmap of the project lists the next steps. These include extending
lead interfaces automatically, moving the remaining Green's-function
transport onto the sparse factorization, energy-parallel transport, import
and export of Kwant and PythTB models, and Hartree--Fock on Bloch meshes.
